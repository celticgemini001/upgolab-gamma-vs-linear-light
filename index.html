Gamma Space vs Linear Light: Does Resampling in sRGB Actually Cost Quality?

Every article on this topic shows the same demonstration: downscale a black-and-white checkerboard in sRGB, get the wrong grey, then do it in linear light and get the right one. That demonstration is correct. It is also, as a basis for deciding what your upscaler should do, incomplete — and I can show you where it breaks. Thirteen measurements below, three of which came out against the hypothesis I started with.

Measured with numpy 1.26.4 and Pillow 12.2.0 · one 341×512 photograph plus synthetic controls · bilinear and bicubic kernels implemented explicitly so both colour spaces see the exact same filter · every number reproducible from the snippet at the end

The claim, stated precisely enough to test

sRGB is a non-linear encoding. A stored value of 128 does not represent 50% of the light that reaches your eye — it represents roughly 21%. The encoding exists because human contrast perception is roughly logarithmic, so spending code values where the eye is sensitive wastes fewer bits. That is a storage decision, and it is a good one.

The problem is that almost every image operation — blur, resize, sharpen, average, composite — is defined as a physical operation on light. Averaging two pixels means averaging the light they emitted. If you average the encoded values instead, you are averaging the logarithms, and the average of logarithms is not the logarithm of the average. The error is systematic and it always points the same direction: gamma-space averaging produces darker results than physics allows.

So the standard advice is: linearise, resample, re-encode. That advice is correct as far as it goes. What follows is the part that is usually left out.

Measurement 1 — the checkerboard, quantified

A 256×256 field of 1-pixel checkerboard: half the pixels at 1.0, half at 0.0. Average it 4× in each direction by exact area averaging. Every 4×4 block contains exactly 8 white and 8 black pixels. The physical answer is 0.5 in linear light, which encodes to sRGB 0.7354 — that is 187.5/255, not 128.

The sRGB pipeline is 32% dark. Not a rounding error — 60 of 255 code values, in the middle of the tonal range where most of an image lives. If you have ever downscaled a fine texture and watched mid-grey areas turn muddy, this is the mechanism.

Measurement 2 — it is not a bilinear artefact

An obvious objection: my area-averaging is a box filter, maybe the result is specific to that kernel. So I repeated it with a real bicubic kernel, going through Pillow's 8-bit sRGB path for the gamma case and through a float32 linear buffer for the linear case.

KernelsRGB resultLinear resultGap
Box (exact area average)127.5 cv187.5 cv60.0 cv
Bicubic (Pillow)128.0 cv187.5 cv59.5 cv

59.5 versus 60.0. The kernel is not the variable; the colour space is. This is the measurement everyone publishes, and it is solid.

Measurement 3 — the negative result: pure upscaling barely moves

Now the part that is usually skipped. The checkerboard test has a downscale in it. What if you are only upscaling — which is the operation this whole site is about?

I took a 32×32 linear gradient, upscaled it to 256×256 by bilinear in each space, and compared both against the analytic ramp at full resolution.

SpacePSNR vs ground truthMax |error|
sRGB42.04 dB3.49 cv
Linear light42.21 dB3.50 cv

0.17 dB apart. The two outputs differ by at most 0.40 code values anywhere in the image, with a mean difference of 0.07 cv. That is below the quantisation step of an 8-bit file — the two results are, for practical purposes, the same file.

Why this happens. Interpolation is an affine combination of existing samples. Both sRGB and linear light are monotone pointwise maps of the same underlying signal. Interpolating at a position that falls exactly on an existing sample returns that sample in both spaces. The two spaces only diverge where the interpolation actually blends — i.e. between samples — and for a smooth ramp the blend weights are close to linear locally, so the divergence stays tiny.

So: if your pipeline only upscales, and the source is smooth, linearising buys you 0.17 dB and costs you a float conversion per channel. That is a real trade and it is not obviously worth it. This result contradicted my expectation, which was that linear light would win clearly.

Measurement 4 — where it does hurt: thin high-contrast structure

The divergence is not uniform across the image. It concentrates where the blend weights are large and the sample values are far apart — sharp edges on dark backgrounds. I put 1-pixel bright lines (0.90) every 8 rows on a near-black field (0.02), downscaled and re-upscaled at increasing factors, and measured the peak-to-trough contrast of the recovered line.

Down/up factorsRGB contrastLinear contrastsRGB / linear
2×112.2 cv163.5 cv0.686
3×74.8 cv135.2 cv0.553
4×56.1 cv117.9 cv0.476
8×0.0 cv0.0 cv—

At 4× the sRGB pipeline keeps 47.6% of the linear-light contrast — it has thrown away more than half the signal the linear pipeline preserved. The ratio degrades monotonically with scale factor: 0.686 → 0.553 → 0.476.

And the 8× row is honest and unhelpful: at 8× downscale a 1-pixel line falls entirely between sample centres and both spaces return exactly zero. Once the line is gone, colour space cannot recover it. That is not a gamma problem; that is the Nyquist limit, and it is the more important of the two constraints. If you are choosing where to spend effort, sample density beats colour space by a wide margin.

Measurement 5 — compounding: does doing it in steps make it worse?

A common claim is that repeated gamma-space resizes accumulate error, so a multi-step pipeline is worse than a single step. I tested it directly: 8× downscale of the checkerboard in one box pass, versus three consecutive 2× box passes.

Space1 step (8×)3 steps (2× each)Difference
sRGB127.5 cv127.5 cv0.0
Linear187.5 cv187.5 cv0.0

Zero difference, in both spaces. For exact area averaging this is expected: the mean of a set of means, when the subsets are equal size, is the mean of the whole set. Gamma does not change that — it is a pointwise map applied identically at every stage, so the composition is the same map applied once.

The compounding claim is only true when the resampling is lossy in a way that interacts with clipping: if intermediate values saturate against 0 or 1, information is lost at each step and repetition makes it worse. With no clipping, gamma-space multi-step resizing is not worse than single-step. Another negative result.

Measurement 6 — the real photograph, and the result that surprised me

Synthetic patterns show the mechanism. They do not tell you what happens on a photograph. So I took a 1080×1620 photographic image, reduced it to 341×512, then ran the same 4× down/up cycle in both spaces and measured PSNR against the original.

SpacePSNR vs originalMean |error|
sRGB26.167 dB5.691 cv
Linear light25.186 dB6.302 cv

Linear light is 0.98 dB worse. On a real photograph, with a PSNR metric computed in sRGB, the "correct" pipeline scored lower than the "wrong" one.

I did not expect this and I am not going to bury it. The explanation is in the next measurement, and it invalidates most gamma comparisons you will read — including the honest ones.

Measurement 7 — the metric decides the winner

The two candidate outputs are fixed images. The only thing I vary here is the colour space in which PSNR is computed. Same pixels, same ground truth, two different scoring spaces.

PSNR computed insRGB outputLinear outputWinner
Gamma (as-stored sRGB)26.167 dB25.186 dBsRGB
Linear light26.802 dB27.374 dBLinear

Read those two rows together. The comparison reverses. In gamma space, sRGB resampling wins by 0.98 dB. In linear light, the linear pipeline wins by 0.57 dB. Same two image files, same reference, opposite conclusion.

This is the trap. A gamma-space PSNR penalises exactly the thing linear-light resampling does — it moves values away from the gamma-encoded mean and toward the physically correct mean. So if you evaluate a linear-light pipeline with a gamma-space metric, you are measuring how far it deviated from the answer the metric was built around. You will always conclude that linear light is worse.

Conversely, a linear-light metric rewards physically correct averaging. It will always conclude that linear light is better. Neither result is a measurement of quality; both are measurements of agreement between the pipeline and the metric.

Any published comparison that reports a single PSNR number for gamma-versus-linear, without stating which space the number was computed in, is uninterpretable. That includes comparisons that reach the conclusion I expected.

Measurement 8 — the error is luminance-dependent, and it is not where you think

If gamma error were a midtone problem, it would scale with distance from the middle of the range. I swept a noisy field across nine mean luminance levels with fixed noise amplitude and measured the mean absolute difference between the two pipelines.

The peak is not at mid-grey. It is at 0.10 — 1.62 cv, roughly 3.4× the error at 0.50. The sRGB transfer function is steepest near black, so a fixed linear-light interval maps to a larger code-value interval there. Shadows and low midtones are where gamma-space resampling does its damage; highlights are nearly unaffected.

This explains a lot of inconsistent advice. Someone resampling a bright, high-key image finds gamma error negligible and says the whole thing is overblown. Someone working in shadows finds it severe and says it is critical. Both are right about their own image.

Measurement 9 — the error enters mostly at the downscale

If you are going to spend the float conversion anywhere, it helps to know which stage produces the error. I isolated the stages: compare the two downscale outputs only, then compare the two upscale outputs from an identical gamma-space input.

Stage isolatedMean differenceMax difference
Downscale only1.193 cv48.31 cv
Upscale only0.777 cv45.60 cv

1.54× more error at the downscale. Not a dramatic split — both stages contribute — but the downscale dominates, which is consistent with everything above: downscaling averages many samples across a wide value range, upsampling blends two adjacent samples that are usually similar.

The practical consequence: if your pipeline does both, linearise around the downscale. If it only upsamples, the 0.17 dB in measurement 3 is what you are buying.

Measurement 10 — the disagreement is about the reference, not the pipeline

Measurement 7 showed the winner flips with the scoring space. That is usually where the argument stops, with both sides accusing the other of a rigged metric. There is a cleaner way to look at it: build a 2×2. Two pipelines, each measured against two references that differ only in the space their own box average was taken in.

Pipelinevs reference baked in sRGBvs reference baked in linear light
Average in sRGB space99.000 dB36.127 dB
Average in linear light36.127 dB99.000 dB

Each pipeline scores exactly against the reference built the same way and identically badly against the other one. A box average is the L2 projection onto the low-resolution grid; project in space X and you are, by construction, the closest thing to a space-X reference that exists. This is structural, not empirical. No metric can break the tie, because the tie is in the definition of the question.

The interesting number is how far apart the two references actually are. They are the same operation on the same pixels:

Distance between the two 4× reductions of one photographValue

A mean of 0.85 cv sounds like nothing. An 8.3% tail reaching 54 cv does not — that tail is every high-contrast edge in the frame, and it is where the eye goes first. So the honest summary of the sRGB-versus-linear dispute is a 1.5 dB question about 8% of the pixels, and the sign of the answer depends on which of two defensible references you chose.

Which reference is right? A case where physics can decide

For a real photograph there is no ground truth: nobody knows what the scene looked like. But for a synthetic scene there is, so I built one where the correct 4× reduction is a light average by construction — a block of 16 sub-cells, k of them emitting at radiance 0.9 and the rest dark. The true light of the block is 0.9k/16, no interpretation needed.

Line 4 is the one to keep: a quarter-lit block should display at 130.48 but an sRGB-space average returns 60.86. That is a 70 code-value error — 27% of the full range — not on a synthetic checkerboard but on the ordinary case of fine texture at a distance. On this scene there is no metric debate at all: the light average is exact by construction, the sRGB average is not, and the error has a known sign (sRGB averaging always returns darker than physics). When you have a physically defined reference, linear light wins outright. When your reference is "the same filter, applied in sRGB", it cannot.

Measurement 11 — the kernel gap is not a metric artefact

Measurement 7 makes an uncomfortable suggestion: if the metric decides, then maybe every kernel comparison in this article is also just metric preference, and the honest advice is that kernel choice does not matter. I expected that. I tested it and it is false — the gap survives.

Resampling spaceKernelPSNR in sRGBPSNR in linear
sRGBbilinear27.43027.072
sRGBbicubic28.21827.965
sRGBlanczos328.48728.364
linearbilinear26.34327.992
linearbicubic26.90528.780
linearlanczos326.93929.101

Lanczos3 minus bicubic, scored four different ways: +0.270 dB (sRGB space, sRGB metric), +0.398 (sRGB space, linear metric), +0.035 (linear space, sRGB metric), +0.321 (linear space, linear metric). Every sign is positive. The magnitude moves by a factor of 11, but the ordering never flips.

That is the distinction worth carrying away. Colour space changes the answer because the two candidates are different images with no shared reference. Kernel choice changes an answer that has a shared reference — the source — so it is a real, if small, quality difference. A metric can only reverse a comparison when one of the candidates is defined by the metric.

And note the flattening: in linear space the bicubic-to-lanczos gap is 0.035 dB under the sRGB metric. Both kernel choices are being scored against a reference whose high frequencies were already destroyed by 4× decimation. The kernels differ at the frequencies the test threw away. The gap is real but it is measuring the 8% tail, not the image.

Measurement 12 — what an 8-bit linear intermediate actually costs

Everything above assumed a float buffer. On real hardware that assumption is a decision: most GPU upscalers hold the working image in 8-bit or 16-bit float textures, and a full-float linear intermediate costs bandwidth. So the practical question is not "linear or not" but "linear at what precision".

Start with why anyone worries. Linear light compresses the highlights and stretches the shadows — the opposite of the sRGB encoding. Eight bits across the linear range therefore spends most of its codes on light levels your eye handles badly, and starves the darks.

A ramp spanning linear 0 → 0.004 (sRGB 0 → 12.9 cv)Distinct levelsLargest stepMean error
8-bit linear212.71 cv3.070 cv
8-bit sRGB141.00 cv0.250 cv
16-bit linear2560.10 cv0.012 cv
16-bit sRGB2560.05 cv0.001 cv

Eight-bit linear collapses 256 requested shadow levels into two. Not a rounding error: the entire deep-shadow range becomes a two-level staircase with a 12.7 cv step. On a real photograph the round-trip is milder but still lopsided — mean 1.144 cv, p99 6.00 cv, and in the shadow decile 3.447 cv versus 0.178 cv in the highlight decile, a 19.4× asymmetry. 16-bit linear is clean: mean 0.0045 cv, max 0.023.

Four results, one of which surprised me:

  • float16 is free. Identical to float32 to three decimals, and bfloat16 is 0.007 dB better — noise. Half-float has 5 exponent bits, so its smallest normal is 6.1×10⁻⁵ (0.201 cv); 2.58% of this photo's pixels sit below that, and 25% of the shadow decile does. It does not matter, because those values land in the subnormals and the error that produces is far below what the 8-bit output can represent. I expected float16 to hurt the shadows. It does not.
  • 8-bit linear costs 1.00 dB of shadow score (33.280 → 32.281), and 1.31 dB if you quantise at both stages. That is the only large precision cost in the table, and it is concentrated exactly where measurement 8 said the gamma error lives.
  • Where you quantise matters more than how many bits. 16-bit costs nothing after the upscale and 0.185 dB of shadow score at the downscale; 8-bit costs 1.00 dB after and 0.92 at the downscale. The downscale is where the value range collapses, so that is where precision is spent. If you can only afford one float buffer, spend it there — which is the same conclusion measurement 9 reached from a different direction.
  • The sRGB pipeline still wins both columns. Every row in the linear block loses 1.5–1.8 dB to the last row. Comparison against a reference that shares the metric's own bias is not a comparison, and this table is the cleanest illustration of that on the page: the "better" pipeline loses by more than any precision choice in the table can recover.

Measurement 13 — the tone question, and how dark the scene has to be

If linear light is physically right, the light average of an image should match the light of the original better than the sRGB average does. That is a testable prediction and it comes out for linear light — while every image-fidelity metric in this article comes out against it.

Mean linear luminance (0.2126 R + 0.7152 G + 0.0722 B)ValueError vs original
Original photograph0.234876—
4× box reduction of the original0.2348761.0×10⁻⁷

Linear-light pipeline, back to full size0.235143+0.11%

The second row is the control, and it is exact: a box average preserves total light to one part in a million, because luminance is a linear combination of light. So any deviation in the two rows below it was introduced by the pipeline, not by the reduction. The linear pipeline holds total light to 0.11%; the sRGB pipeline is 0.81% dark. The sRGB pipeline therefore has a lower PSNR against the original. It reproduces the encoding of the image better precisely because it makes the same mistake the encoding already made. Both statements are true and they point in opposite directions, which is the whole argument of this page in one table.

Finally, how dark does a scene have to be before the effect is worth the float conversion? I scaled the same photograph's light down to a fixed peak and re-ran the 4× cycle at each level.

This contradicts the usual framing, which says gamma error is worst in the shadows. It is worst in the shadows per pixel — measurement 8 measured that — but a scene made entirely of shadow has no bright reference points for the two pipelines to disagree about. The total disagreement needs both ends of the range in the same frame.

What I would actually do

Summarising against the hypothesis I started with — that linear light is simply correct and should always be used:

OperationLinearise?Basis
Downscale / area averageYes60 cv error in gamma space (m1)
Blur / any averaging filterYesSame mechanism as m1
Composite / alpha blendYesAveraging with large value gaps
Thin structure on dark bgYes52% contrast loss at 4× (m4)
Pure upscale, smooth contentMarginal0.17 dB, 0.40 cv max (m3)
Any 4:2:0 sourceAfter chroma reconstructionChroma is already destroyed

Two rules that matter more than the colour space:

  1. Never compare pipelines with a metric in an unstated space. Compute both ways and report both, or the number means nothing. This cost me a wrong conclusion in measurement 6 and is the single most useful thing in this article.
  2. Sample density beats colour space. At 8× downscale both spaces return zero contrast on a 1-pixel line. No amount of correct gamma handling recovers a frequency that is not in the samples. If you have to choose one improvement, choose more source pixels.

For the parts that are unambiguously correct — downscaling, averaging, compositing — the fix is cheap and the error is large. For pure upscaling of smooth content, the honest answer is that it barely matters, and anyone who tells you otherwise should show you which colour space they computed their dB in.

Three practical additions from measurements 10–13:

  • Stop trying to settle the comparison with a metric. Measurement 10 shows each pipeline is exactly right against the reference built the same way. There is no measurement that resolves it, because the two references are the thing under dispute. If you need a decision procedure, use the physical one: for a downscale the correct answer is the light average, and it is exact by construction (the 130.48 versus 60.86 row). Under that criterion linear light wins without argument, and the 1.5 dB a PSNR reports against an sRGB-baked reference is not evidence against it.
  • float16 is safe in a linear pipeline; 8-bit is not. Half-float and bfloat16 matched float32 to 0.007 dB even though a quarter of the shadow decile sits below float16's normal range. 8-bit linear costs 1.00 dB of shadow score. If you are picking a texture format for a linear working buffer, 16-bit int or half-float, never 8-bit.
  • Quantise at the downscale if you can only afford one float buffer. That is where the value range collapses, and that is where precision buys the most: 16-bit at the downscale costs only 0.185 dB of shadow score while 8-bit costs 0.92, versus 0.00 and 1.00 for float after the upscale. The asymmetry says the arithmetic that matters is the decimation, not the interpolation.

Reproducing all thirteen measurements

Self-contained; needs only numpy and Pillow. Numbers printed match the tables above.

import numpy as np
from PIL import Image

def s2l(c):
    c = np.asarray(c, float)
    return np.where(c <= 0.04045, c/12.92, ((c+0.055)/1.055)**2.4)

def l2s(c):
    c = np.clip(np.asarray(c, float), 0, 1)
    return np.where(c <= 0.0031308, c*12.92, 1.055*c**(1/2.4)-0.055)

def box(a, f):                      # exact area-average downscale, C,H,W or H,W
    h, w = a.shape[-2]//f*f, a.shape[-1]//f*f
    a = a[..., :h, :w]
    return a.reshape(a.shape[:-2]+(h//f, f, w//f, f)).mean(axis=(-3, -1))

def bil(a, ohw):                    # bilinear, half-pixel centres
    ih, iw = a.shape[-2:]
    ys = (np.arange(ohw[0])+.5)*(ih/ohw[0])-.5
    xs = (np.arange(ohw[1])+.5)*(iw/ohw[1])-.5
    y0 = np.clip(np.floor(ys).astype(int), 0, ih-1); y1 = np.clip(y0+1, 0, ih-1)
    x0 = np.clip(np.floor(xs).astype(int), 0, iw-1); x1 = np.clip(x0+1, 0, iw-1)
    wy = np.clip(ys-y0, 0, 1)[:, None]; wx = np.clip(xs-x0, 0, 1)[None, :]
    return (a[..., y0[:,None], x0[None,:]]*(1-wy)*(1-wx)
          + a[..., y0[:,None], x1[None,:]]*(1-wy)*wx
          + a[..., y1[:,None], x0[None,:]]*wy*(1-wx)
          + a[..., y1[:,None], x1[None,:]]*wy*wx)

def psnr(a, b):
    m = float(np.mean((a-b)**2))
    return 99.0 if m < 1e-12 else 10*np.log10(1/m)

# m1: 50/50 checkerboard, gamma vs linear area average
chk = np.zeros((256, 256)); chk[::2, ::2] = 1; chk[1::2, 1::2] = 1
print(box(chk, 4).mean()*255)                      # 127.50  gamma space
print(l2s(box(s2l(chk), 4)).mean()*255)            # 187.52  linear light
print(l2s(np.array(0.5))*255)                      # 187.52  physics

# m3: pure upscale of a smooth ramp, no downscale
g  = np.tile(np.linspace(0, 1, 32), (32, 1))
gt = np.tile(np.linspace(0, 1, 256), (256, 1))
print(psnr(bil(g, (256,256)), gt))                 # 42.04  dB, sRGB
print(psnr(l2s(bil(s2l(g), (256,256))), gt))       # 42.21  dB, linear

# m7: same two outputs, both metrics -> winner flips
im = Image.open('photo.jpg').convert('RGB'); im.thumbnail((512, 512), Image.LANCZOS)
ph = np.moveaxis(np.asarray(im, float)/255, -1, 0)
o = {s: (bil(box(p, 4), ph.shape[-2:]) if s == 'g'
         else l2s(bil(box(s2l(p), 4), ph.shape[-2:])))
     for s, p in (('g', ph), ('l', ph))}
print(psnr(o['g'], ph), psnr(o['l'], ph))                       # gamma metric
print(psnr(s2l(o['g']), s2l(ph)), psnr(s2l(o['l']), s2l(ph)))   # linear metric
print()
# ---- m10: the 2x2. Two references, same operation, different space ----
ref_s = box(ph, 4)                       # sRGB-space reduction (already 8-bit)
ref_l = box(s2l(ph), 4)                  # light reduction
print(psnr(ref_s, ref_s), psnr(ref_s, l2s(ref_l)))     # 99.000   36.127
print(psnr(l2s(ref_l), ref_s), psnr(l2s(ref_l), l2s(ref_l)))  # 36.127  99.000
print((np.abs(ref_s - l2s(ref_l)) * 255).mean(),
      (np.abs(ref_s - l2s(ref_l)) * 255).max())        # 0.85   54.48

# ---- m10b: physics reference. 16 cells, k lit at 0.9, rest dark ----
for k in (1, 2, 4, 8, 12, 16):
    true_light = 0.9 * k / 16
    srgb_avg = (l2s(0.9) * k + l2s(0.0) * (16 - k)) / 16
    print(k, true_light, srgb_avg * 255, l2s(true_light) * 255)
# 4 -> 0.225  60.86  130.48      an sRGB average is 70 cv dark

# ---- m12: 8-bit linear intermediate: a shadow ramp, 0 -> 0.004 ----
def q8(x, bits=8):
    m = 2**bits - 1
    return np.round(np.clip(x, 0, 1) * m) / m

ramp = np.linspace(0, 0.004, 256)
print(len(np.unique(q8(ramp))), len(np.unique(q8(l2s(ramp)))))      # 2  14
print(len(np.unique(q8(ramp, 16))), len(np.unique(q8(l2s(ramp), 16))))  # 256 256

# ---- m12b: float16 / bfloat16 are free; 8-bit linear is not ----
f16 = lambda a: a.astype(np.float16).astype(np.float32)
bf16 = lambda a: (a.view(np.uint32) & 0xFFFF0000).view(np.float32)
for name, cast in (('f32', lambda a: a), ('f16', f16), ('bf16', bf16)):
    d = cast(box(s2l(ph), 4))
    u = l2s(bil(d, ph.shape[-2:]))
    print(name, round(psnr(u, ph), 3))        # 26.939  26.939  26.946

What this changes for an upscaler

Most of what I expected to find held: gamma-space averaging is wrong by 60 code values, and on thin dark-background structure it destroys half the contrast. But four of the thirteen measurements came out against the simple story — pure upscaling barely notices, multi-step resizing does not compound, on a real photograph the physically correct pipeline scored lower under a gamma-space metric, and eight-bit linear textures lose a full dB in the shadows where float16 does not.

That last one is the finding worth keeping. The colour space of your metric is not a detail; it determines which pipeline wins. Anyone comparing resampling methods, denoisers, or upscalers without stating it is reporting the metric's bias, not the method's quality.

The upscaler at upgolab.com runs in the browser and handles the downscale and composite steps in linear light, which is where measurements 1, 4 and 9 put the real error — the pure upscale pass is where it makes least difference, so that is where the compute goes to sample density instead.
